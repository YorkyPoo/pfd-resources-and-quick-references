The Acronyn

Beginning the Simulation

Whenever performing a simulation exercise, the first step is often the most difficult. Where do you start? The following pages have some acronyms and tools for getting yourself organized during a simulation. Again, you may use all or part of the systems below. Whatever you do, have a system to track your resources and tactics that is easy to replicate and give to an assessor or Incident Commander who assumes command from you.

The acronym SAW CSS RECEO VSS can be used to get you started during the actual simulation exercise and in the real world. You will have a template from which to set up a system. Having a system will prevent you from forgetting anything and leaving points on the floor. This contains all the essential elements of tactics and strategy that must be addressed and verbalized during a simulation exercise. This has been used in assessment center simulations by thousands of firefighters with remarkable results.

More importantly, this also works for the real world and helps build muscle memory and systems that you can apply consistently in both arenas. This is not check-box firefighting. As you will see, we have synthesized crucial factors into a concise system that blends strategy and tactics, the Incident Command System, and Standard Operating Guidelines. As we have mentioned, the vast body of knowledge that encompasses emergency operations is enormous and requires lifelong study. No tactical worksheet or Standard Operating Guidelines alone, no matter how sophisticated, will replace a thinking officer who can size up a dynamic situation.

If you have your own acronym or system, then use whatever is most comfortable. Make sure that all of the elements below are reflected, however. Once again, do not leave points behind. We will first explain this system and then show how to use it during an actual simulation, when your nerves are competing with your brain.

To remember this acronym, simply practice saying it over and over. Some firefighters pronounce SAW CSS RECEO VSS as Saw, sees, receo, vees to make it easier to remember. Once you study the components, you will easily memorize it since it is a derivative of RECEOVS, which has been around for decades. Even if you have never heard of any of this before, with some practice, experience, and common sense, it will come naturally.

Structure Fires



Structure Fires

Size-up.

Size-up is the mental evaluation of the situation using FPODP. This must be performed first to set up your plan properly. This must also be done very quickly, but as accurately as possible with limited information. Size up is continual. You must verbalize that you would perform a 360-degree lap of the building, or assign another company to give you a report on conditions from the rear. Do not forget the rear. Many candidates forget to verbalize this critical step.

Ensure that you consider all seven sides of the building: four sides, top, bottom, and inside. Look at construction, building stability, and level of involvement. Consider avenue and direction of fire spread. Talk to any bystanders or reporting party and find out the victim profile. This is a critical step in any incident, especially structure fires. Here, you will assess if civilian life is at risk and thus determine the amount of risk that your firefighters should take.

There are four levels of victim profile: low, moderate, high, and confirmed. This progressive level of victim presence yields a progressive level of risk you are willing to take.

Low victim profile is for a building in which all victims are reported out via a reliable source or the building is likely vacant or unused. Low does not mean we do not search, but low gain mandates low risk. A low victim profile with heavy involvement may warrant a defensive strategy, while a moderate or confirmed victim profile with heavy involvement may warrant a high level of risk, which would drive an offensive strategy, and hence, offensive tactics.

Moderate victim profile would be a fire in which no one is present or if anyone is reported inside. You may enter based on structural stability and if there is a credible reporting of a high likelihood of victims. Statements like, I think someone is home but I have not seen them come outside, would warrant a moderate profile.

High victim profile is for scenarios with a high profile, such as a residence or apartment at two in the morning with vehicles out front. Also, the scenario itself could warrant a high profile.

A confirmed victim profile would be a scenario in which a credible bystander or reporting party tells you that indeed victims are trapped or you see victims trapped. Statements like, My husband is in there, stated by a wife out front would be a credible confirmed victim profile.

FPODP example for first-arriving engine.

Facts: One-story home with fire from the A and B corner garage.

Probabilities: Possible fire extension into the home and attic space. Possibly occupied.

Own situation: I am the first-arriving engine on a residential alarm assignment.

Decision: We will use an offensive strategy.

Plan of action: I will take a lap, have my firefighter stretch a line to the A-side front door and the garage while preparing to enter. My engineer will secure the hydrant next door. I will pass command to Engine 2 who will deploy a back-up line and establish water supply. Truck 1 will search. Truck 2 will prepare to vertically vent.

A key point as the first-arriving company officer is to verbalize what your crew is doing. Just saying what you are doing is not enough. You cannot take for granted what they are doing and forget to assign them. A great place to do this is in the plan of operations part of FPODP.

FPODP by first-arriving chief officer example.

Facts: One-story home with fire from the A and B corner garage and attic space. Possibly occupied.

Probabilities: Possible fire extension into the home and living space.

Own situation: I am the first-arriving chief officer on scene with a residential alarm assignment with one engine on scene and three engines, two trucks, and a medic unit arriving.

Decision: We will use an offensive strategy.

Plan of action: Engine 2 will assume water supply. Truck 1 will search. Truck 2 will ventilate. The chief officer took a broader view of the incident, building upon the first-arriving engine's plan, setting up for rapid intervention, medical, and used key points to verbalize.

Key points to verbalize are: FPODP, Seven Sides, Construction Type, 360-degree lap, Contact the reporting party, Victim Profile, and Arrival report.

Apparatus placement.

If safely possible, verbalize that you would drive past the structure to see three sides and get as much information as available. Before you step out of the cab, you will have seen three sides of the problem. Consider other apparatus, such as truck companies. Hoses can be extended, whereas ladders have tighter limitations. Also consider access and egress of ambulances and room for supply lines. Stay out of the hot zone, where any of the following may be adding danger: collapse, heat, hazardous materials, being downwind or downhill, and power lines. Key points to verbalize are apparatus placement past three sides and hot zone considerations.

Water supply.

You must be able to support your operation. As the first-in officer, you either have it at the scene by virtue of a hydrant or enough water in your tank, are getting it by laying in or out, or need it by having someone bring it to you. Consider different fire flow formulas to determine the gallons per minute required to extinguish the fire. The Iowa fire flow formula is an easy, scientific way to estimate fire flow: Length times Width times Height divided by 100. In addition, consider fixed systems like sprinklers, standpipes, and the fire department connection. Key points to verbalize include who is responsible, what is the type of supply, where is the source, and fire department connection if present.

Command.

You must rapidly assess whether you will assume command based on your department's standard operating guidelines. Some agencies require the first-arriving officer to assume and maintain command, while others may allow the first-arriving officer to pass command to the next officer and initiate fire attack. As a general rule for the Incident Command System, you must be on scene to assume command. Key points to verbalize are who is taking command or passing it, what is the incident name, and where is the Incident Command Post location.

Strategy.

Verbalize if you will use an offensive, defensive, or combination strategy based on your size-up and risk-gain analysis. This is the go or no-go based on victim profile and threat potential to your firefighters. Consider collapse potential, construction type, lightweight construction, unsupported mansards, roof involvement and construction type, exposures, basements, hillsides, accelerants, active shooters, and a host of other factors. Remember, the most crucial decision on every fire is the strategic go or no-go. Many firefighters have been killed and injured due to failure by incident commanders and first-arriving officers to properly make this critical life decision and re-evaluate it. Do not just run in blindly. Every structure fire warrants a size-up, including a lap as soon as possible and a victim profile assessment no matter how routine it may seem. House fires are the most lethal. They are not routine anymore, nor is anything we respond to in this day and age.

A combination strategy may be warranted when you have a portion of buildings involved with one that is tenable and others that are not. You may also have a fire with multiple offensive and defensive spaces. Ensure all resources on scene know the demarcation line. Key points to verbalize are low, moderate, high, or confirmed victim profile.

Safety.

Once the strategy is set, you can then take appropriate safety measures. Safety considerations include SCBA and personal protective equipment, rapid intervention teams, and collapse potential. Ensure that defensive boundaries are clear. Safety considerations also include remaining out of collapse zones and ensuring crews wear SCBA even in defensive positions. Ensure accountability so members do not inadvertently enter offensive spaces during a defensive operation. Ensure that you have a reliable accountability system for your incident. Use the Incident Command System to track your tactical supervisors. A tactical command board may be utilized as a redundant accountability system at the command post. Key points to verbalize are rapid intervention teams, two-out or backup lines, personnel accountability reports, and utility control.

Rescue.

The first tactical priority is rescue, although hoselines in place may be required to accomplish this. For example, a transitional attack may be warranted to allow interior attack and search crews to advance. If you have a confirmed or high victim profile, remember the victim profiling acronym SIGNAL: Species, Incapacity, Gender, Number, Age, and Location. Just ten seconds invested in getting a SIGNAL from a bystander will pay huge dividends. Key points to verbalize are primary search complete, secondary search complete, all clear, and medical treatment and transport.

Primary and secondary search should be done by different crews to ensure a fresh set of eyes has completed each task. Have medical treatment and transport capabilities on scene before getting a victim out of the building. Carbon monoxide binds to blood hemoglobin over 200 times faster than oxygen. Getting them out of the building is not enough. They must have advanced life support and emergency room care as soon as possible. Always have one transport unit standing by for firefighters or unknown victims who are suddenly found.

Exposures.

Exposures must be addressed both internally and externally. Avoid tunnel vision on the seat of the fire. Step back to see the big picture and stop the fire's forward progress into exposures. Consider internal exposures like attics, cocklofts, voids, firewalls, and roof assemblies. Say you have a house fire. Without a 360-degree lap, it would be tempting to assume the home is fully involved and deploy the first line to the exposure. In fact, there may be a tenable space in the involved home. With a victim profile of moderate or higher, this would warrant deploying the first line into the home. It all depends upon the victim profile. Key points to verbalize are internal exposures and external exposures.

Confinement.

You may use the acronym SLICE-RS for initial attack: Size-up, Location of fire, Isolate flow path, Cool from a safe distance, Extinguish, Rescue, and Salvage. Modern fire behavior studies emphasize the importance of flow path. Simply opening a door can create a flow path and intensify the fire. Controlling flow path is essential to keeping the fire contained. Key points to verbalize are location of hoselines and flow path control.

Extinguishment.

Extinguishment provides a compliment to confinement. The size and number of hoselines you have chosen matters. Do not make the big mistake of using bread-and-butter house fire tactics on commercial occupancies. The Iowa fire flow formula will help: Length times Width times Height divided by 100. Key points to verbalize are size of hoselines, number of hoselines, and overall flow required.

Overhaul.

Verbalize who is performing the overhaul and in what locations. For example, in a balloon frame building, you would check extension in the walls and attic appropriately. Always check above the fire in concealed spaces and cocklofts. Using a thermal imaging camera is not enough. Open walls and ceilings. Remove insulation. Consider Class A foam. Consider leaving the area of origin intact for the investigator within reason prior to overhauling and potentially ruining any evidence. Key points to verbalize are who is performing overhaul, and key areas and priorities for overhaul.

Ventilation.

Controlling ventilation is just as important due to flow path and increasing fire activity. The purpose of the ventilation must be stated. Are you venting for life, for extinguishment, or for confinement? Venting for life involves rapid removal of heat and smoke to increase a tenable environment. Venting to cool involves removal of heat for hose teams to enter. Venting to confine may involve vertical ventilation to prevent lateral spread. You must verbalize the location and nature of the ventilation. Ventilation and fire attack must be well coordinated. Key points to verbalize are purpose of vent, nature of vent, and coordination of vent.

Salvage.

State your priorities. For example, a two-story building with fire on the second floor would require the first floor to be a priority salvage location due to potential water damage. Contact the reporting party or owner and support their needs. Ensure you know of any belongings of value that have been identified. Key points to verbalize are contact the reporting party, where and what is the priority, and who is performing salvage.

Support.

Additional resources to support the operation must be called ahead of time. Anticipate your needs before they arise. If you have a working fire, call an ambulance. Verbalize that they are there to provide any required treatment and transport. This shows you are thinking ahead. Resources to consider calling include alarms, personnel, special equipment, and other agencies.

Using these tools on a test.

During your test prep time, set up a tactical worksheet using SAW CSS RECEO VSS down the left side of your paper. As you write these tactical objectives down, create a checklist to not forget anything. Keep track of your resources using your local Incident Command System early. This will help you keep a manageable span of control. When the assessors give you the companies on your second and third alarms, just write them down and cross them off as you assign them.

When the simulation begins, perform a quick mental size-up out loud using FPODP. Then give an arrival report. From there, simply continue to verbalize your thoughts, reasoning, and actions, and track your resources on your worksheet. Ensure that you are aware of the time that you have. Set your stopwatch to zero and be ready to start it. Remember the importance of training and repetitive practice. Follow the directions. While it is most common to arrive first in, some simulations have you arrive and assume command with companies already on scene working. Practice static, dynamic, first-in, and assume command scenarios. There is absolutely no substitute for hands-on practice.

For high rise fires, the time-tested acronym ALS BASE, which stands for attack, lobby, staging, and base, is a basic foundation to build upon. Consider amending your worksheet to include special tactics like rapid ascent teams, elevator and lobby control, and mechanical ventilation.

Vegetation Fires



Vegetation Fires

Size-up.

Size-up is the mental evaluation of the situation. Ensure that you consider four sides of the incident: north, south, east, and west. The goal is to box in the fire. Exposures could be homes, neighborhoods, roads, or any values at risk. Weather involves relative humidity, wind, and temperature. Stay ahead of the incident power curve.

Apparatus placement.

Stay in the black or burned area. This is the primary safety zone. Try to remain upwind, out of the smoke.

Water supply.

Water tenders are likely the most common water source and can be fed off hydrants or ponds for drafting. Have a water tender for each division.

Command.

You must be on scene to be in command. This means you will potentially be wearing two hats: company officer and Incident Commander. Ensure you verbalize this mobile or working command.

Strategy.

Verbalize if it is offensive, defensive, or combination strategy. An offensive strategy would be attacking the fire with a mobile attack or progressive hose lay. A defensive strategy would be structure defense.

Safety.

The acronym LCES stands for lookouts, communications, escape routes, and safety zones. Be on the lookout for power lines down and snags, which are burned out trees that can fall and severely injure or kill firefighters.

Rescue.

Evacuation is the priority when you have structures at risk. Have an ambulance available on scene.

Exposures.

Structure defense is the primary tactic when structures are threatened. Livestock are also of significant value.

Confinement.

Build a box around the fire on all four sides and contain it. Identify natural barriers and control objectives.

Extinguishment.

Perimeter control is identified by a certain amount of feet from the fire line that the Incident Commander wants to be cold.

Overhaul.

The term mop up is used for overhauling a fire. This means checking for heat, smoking snags, or open flame.

Ventilation.

Ventilation refers to weather conditions. This includes temperature, wind, and relative humidity.

Salvage.

Salvage is likely limited unless structures or other values are involved.

Support.

Anticipate your needs before they arise. Consider calling bulldozers, hand crews, air units, and other agencies.

Multicasualty Incidents

Multicasualty Incidents

Size-up.

Multicasualty incidents have unique characteristics and generally fall into three categories: medical, trauma, and hazardous materials. Identify the number and category of patients early. Patients generally come in four categories: immediate, delayed, minor, and deceased. Modern incidents could involve an active shooter or a type of domestic terrorist act.

Apparatus placement.

A key point is the traffic plan to get ambulances quickly in and out of the scene. Your pickup point will be near the treatment area. Also, plan on a landing zone for air ambulances. For hazmat or active shooter incidents, stay out of the hot zone.

Command.

You may establish unified command with law enforcement, a school, a hospital, or the local hazardous materials authority.

Strategy.

An offensive strategy would be allowed when the threat is neutralized. A combination strategy with hot, warm, and cold zones would be established when the threat poses a hazard to responders.

Safety.

Safety issues come from active shooters, secondary devices, collapse potential, and hazardous materials. A new tactic is the use of rescue task forces where fire, emergency medical services, and law enforcement create task forces to enter the warm zone to treat and extract patients under force protection.

Rescue.

The three primary objectives are triage, treatment, and transport.

Exposures.

Consider downwind exposures and potential contamination of hazardous materials. Provide gross decontamination.

Confinement.

The term walking wounded applies to ambulatory patients who need to be contained so they do not contaminate hospitals or wander off. Set up immediate, delayed, and minor treatment areas.

Extinguishment.

The incident must be mitigated or neutralized.

Overhaul.

Overhaul comes in the form of secondary searches, containing a hazardous material, or searching for secondary devices.

Ventilation.

Ventilation refers to weather conditions. Consider sheltering patients in extreme heat or humidity.

Hazardous Materials Incidents

Hazardous Materials Incidents

Size-up.

Identify the product using a Department of Transportation Emergency Response Guide. Consider weather and wind for evacuation potential.

Apparatus placement.

Stage apparatus upwind and uphill and out of the hot zone.

Safety.

The first priority is to isolate and deny entry. An assistant safety officer is usually required.

Rescue.

Live victims may be immediately rescued when first responders wear appropriate gear. Provide gross decontamination.

Confinement.

Locate, identify, isolate, deny entry, and contain are the fundamental objectives.
